Why Thalyn Exists

I wanted to build a world. Every way in started with a manual.

Not a game.

The honest version, in my own words — including the part I still can't answer on my own.

Not a game. Not a level. A place — somewhere with a shape and a mood, that you could walk into and believe.

Every route there ran through Blender, Unity or Unreal. Serious programs, all of them, each with its own buttons, its own vocabulary, its own scripting language, and months of learning standing between me and the thing I actually wanted to make.

I didn't want to learn three programs. I wanted the world.

Thalyn is the tool I couldn't find.

The part I didn't expect

It started with a conversation about VR.

About why some virtual environments feel like somewhere, and others feel like a room with objects placed in it.

That took me into colour theory, and from there into environmental psychology: prospect and refuge, attention restoration, the landscape preferences that appear to be older than any of us. The same body of research used to design real places that people actually want to be in. If you want that properly, it has its own page.

What struck me was that these things are almost always handled separately. Terrain in one pass. Planting in another. Light and weather bolted on at the end. Nothing anywhere checking that they agree with each other.

So I built something that checks. Every element is scored against the others — whether the shape of the land, what grows on it, where the water sits, the light and the air are all saying the same thing, instead of each one landing in roughly the right area on its own.

Whether it fully works is the part I can't answer by myself.

What I can tell you is that a Thalyn world resolves as one coherent scene rather than a scatter of individually correct objects. Whether the feeling lands the way I intended is a different question, and it needs worlds I didn't build to answer it.

That's the honest reason Thalyn is in Early Access. Not to fund it. To meet worlds I never imagined.

What you make is yours

The licensing on most tools has always frustrated me.

The pattern is familiar. The tool is affordable and the output is hedged: you can use it, but not commercially. Or commercially, but not past a revenue threshold. Or you can for now, until the terms change and what you built is suddenly sitting on the wrong side of a licence.

I think that's backwards. The projects I've learned the most from give things away and get a larger, better ecosystem back. The more that gets shared, the more all of us can build.

So it works like this. Everything you create in Thalyn is yours: commercially, permanently, including if Thalyn ever stops being sold. One purchase. £19.99. No tiers, nothing held back for a higher one. The full licence is in plain English.

The price is deliberately low, and that's not a launch trick. This gets better as more worlds exist, and more worlds only exist if it isn't expensive to be here.

What happens next

What happens next isn't up to me.

Thalyn isn't finished, and it shouldn't be.

Build something. Share the seed — because generation is deterministic, anyone you send it to rebuilds your world exactly, not an approximation of it. Tell me where the generator gets it wrong, which feelings don't land, what you reached for that wasn't there.

I'm one person with one imagination, and I've already found the edge of it. The worlds that decide what Thalyn becomes next won't be mine.

They'll be yours.
